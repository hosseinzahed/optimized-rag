FICTION: Meridian Search and the Missing Archive

Meridian Search was the largest discovery platform in the fictional country of Norland. Its search engine answered billions of questions, from train schedules to recipes, and its archive preserved snapshots of public websites. In 2032, a librarian named Elin Voss discovered that an entire decade of local newspaper pages had nearly vanished from Meridian's results.

The newspapers had not been deleted. Their pages remained available on the websites of the Bracken County Library and several small publishers. But a change in Meridian's indexing system had classified the scanned pages as duplicates because many of them shared the same navigation text. The articles themselves were images, and the indexer had not reliably extracted their text. When residents searched for a 2021 flood or a former mayor's name, unrelated national pages appeared instead.

Elin documented fifty failed searches and sent them to Meridian's public feedback address. The automated response thanked her without offering a case number. She then published a short report describing the problem, including links to the original pages and a list of search phrases. The report reached Tomas Ire, a Meridian engineer working on the archival search team.

Tomas reproduced forty-six of the fifty failures. The other four had already been corrected by a routine recrawl. He opened an internal incident and proposed two changes: separate a page's navigation template from its article content, and run optical character recognition on eligible public scans. His manager approved a limited pilot, but the legal and accessibility teams asked that the company consult the publishers before making archive copies more visible.

The pilot covered three publishers and the Bracken County Library. Each participant could specify which public collections Meridian should index and which pages should be excluded. Meridian agreed to display links to the source site beside every archive result. It also offered a correction form for transcription errors, while making clear that source publishers remained responsible for their own original records.

The first test produced an embarrassing result. The recognition system read a 2021 headline about a flood gate as a headline about a food gate. That mistake made a search for emergency infrastructure less reliable, even though the page was now technically indexed. The team added confidence scores, retained the image alongside the extracted text, and asked volunteer librarians to review a sample of low-confidence pages. Corrections were recorded with their source and review date.

Another issue involved names. A historical article described a person using a name that the person had later changed. Meridian's existing interface displayed a large excerpt even when users searched for the old name. After consulting the library and privacy specialists, the team retained the historically accurate source link but shortened automated excerpts for sensitive personal-name queries. Users could still open the publisher's page to understand the full context.

After four months, Meridian repeated Elin's fifty searches. Forty-eight returned the relevant local page within the first ten results. One remaining failure involved a damaged scan, and the other involved an ambiguous place name. The team published both limitations rather than removing them from the evaluation set. It also established a quarterly review with participating publishers and a public issue tracker for archive indexing defects.

Elin did not consider the work finished. Some papers had not joined the pilot, and better visibility did not guarantee permanent preservation. Still, the project changed Meridian's habits. Search quality was no longer assessed only by whether popular pages appeared quickly. For this collection, the company also measured whether people could find a small community's own record of what had happened.
